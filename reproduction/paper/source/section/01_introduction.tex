\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
A distribution agreement in the Contract Understanding Atticus Dataset (CUAD) \citep{cuad} defines \emph{Trademarks} as only the names listed in Exhibit~A. Elsewhere it requires the distributor to report unauthorized use of the Trademarks. Suppose a contract assistant is asked whether the distributor must report a confusable name that is not in Exhibit~A. A retriever returns the notification clause, which shares words with the question, but the definition sits in another section. In our CUAD retrieval experiments, the retrieved passages contain the needed definition for only 29\% to 44\% of questions across three retrievers. A model that reads the notification clause without the definition takes \emph{trademarks} in its usual sense of any brand name and concludes that the name must be reported. GPT-5.6-terra gives exactly this answer without the definition and the correct one with it (Table~\ref{tab:examples}). The answer sounds careful, and nothing in the retrieved text contradicts it. We call such an answer a \emph{silent misreading}: a wrong answer about a locally defined term, given by the term's usual meaning, with no sign of doubt.

Such errors arise wherever language models read text written for a particular group. Contracts define capitalized terms, protocol documentation fixes the meaning of words such as \emph{pool} or \emph{position}, and online communities maintain glossaries of their own senses \citep{lucy-bamman-glossaries}. These are also the settings in which organizations most often ask models about their own documents. The safeguards that deployed systems rely on, such as agreement across samples, confidence thresholds, and checks against retrieved evidence, all let a silent misreading pass. It also falls outside the categories used to evaluate model errors. Hallucination research separates factuality errors, which contradict world knowledge, from faithfulness errors, which contradict the input \citep{space}. A silent misreading is neither, because the usual meaning is correct in general and the input says nothing against it.

The failure is also distinct from knowledge conflict \citep{longpre-etal-2021-entity,kc-survey,conflictbank,knowledge-conflict-faithfulness}. That work places evidence in the context that contradicts what the model has learned and asks which source the model follows. In a silent misreading the contradicting evidence is absent. When it is supplied, models largely use it. The first problem is therefore to know which local definitions a model must be given, and only then whether it uses them.

We study this problem with controlled evidence of three kinds: human-written redefinition items, a synthetic corpus whose term classes are fixed before any text is generated, and diagnostic questions on contracts and community text whose answer keys we audit against the sources. Four findings emerge.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Following a definition and recognizing that one is missing are separable capabilities} (Sections~\ref{sec:stable}, \ref{sec:detect}, and~\ref{sec:repair}). Frontier models adopt a stated redefinition on 95\% to 99.8\% of human-written items, yet redefined common words are the terms they misread most often without the definition, their own gloss of such a word does not help, and their confidence is close to chance exactly on these words.
\item \textbf{Current safeguards miss the resulting errors} (Section~\ref{sec:detect}). Sampling agreement, stated confidence, and faithfulness-oriented contrastive decoding neither flag nor fix them, because each relies on information that is not in the input.
\item \textbf{Definitions must come from the source} (Section~\ref{sec:repair}). The source definition repairs errors that the model's own gloss leaves or creates, loosely worded glosses harm correct answers, and in contracts the definition alone repairs about half of the errors because it refers to other clauses that must also be retrieved.
\item \textbf{Public benchmarks do not test definition boundaries, and we provide questions that do} (Sections~\ref{sec:predict} and~\ref{sec:repair}). Adding definitions changes public contract benchmarks by at most 1.1 points, while the same contracts produce errors on 13.2\% of diagnostic questions. We release these question sets with an audit of their keys, and a small-model score that ranks difficult items for building and auditing such evaluations.
\end{enumerate}

Together these findings identify a gap in current models and in how they are evaluated. Models can use a local definition they are given, but nothing in their behavior signals that one is needed, so the errors fall outside what benchmarks and runtime safeguards check, in exactly the settings where organizations deploy models on their own documents. Recognizing that a familiar word may carry a local meaning is therefore a natural target for evaluation and training, and until models acquire it, systems that read local text should locate and supply local definitions from the source rather than rely on the model to infer them (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
